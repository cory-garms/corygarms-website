When you push a mobile sensor cart packed with a Livox LiDAR scanner and stereo cameras through a dense conifer forest, your computer has to make sense of absolute chaos.

In our USDA-funded project, **CUTMAP**, the goal is autonomous forest inventory: measuring individual tree diameters at breast height (DBH), height, and 3D lean angles in real time as you walk.

The catch? Nature hates clean geometric primitives.

### Why Standard Circle Fitting Fails

In forestry, the textbook method is simple: take a horizontal slice of your point cloud at 1.3 meters above the ground, fit a 2D circle using least squares, and measure diameter.

That works fine in a manicured orchard. In a real forest:
1. **Stems lean:** If a Douglas-fir tilts even $12^\circ$, slicing it horizontally produces an ellipse rather than a circle, inflating timber volume estimates by up to $14\%$.
2. **Understory clutter:** Low branches, hanging moss, and dense salal brush corrupt standard circle fits, pulling centers off into empty space.
3. **Hardware limits:** You don't have an air-conditioned cloud cluster in the woods. Everything has to solve at 10–15 Hz on an embedded NVIDIA Jetson Orin powered by a battery pack.

### The Fix: Geometry-Constrained RANSAC

Instead of fitting 2D circles, we fit full 3D cylinders defined by an axis vector $\mathbf{a}$, a center point $\mathbf{p}_0$, and a radius $r$.

To keep RANSAC from wasting time evaluating random brush or ground returns, we enforce a strict physical constraint on surface normals: **cylinder surface normals must be perpendicular to the stem axis**:

$$
|\mathbf{n}_i \cdot \mathbf{a}| \le \sin(\theta_{\text{thresh}})
$$

```python
# Sample 2 seed points with divergent normals
axis = np.cross(normal_1, normal_2)
axis /= np.linalg.norm(axis)

# Enforce realistic tree lean prior (reject non-vertical junk)
if abs(axis[2]) < np.cos(np.radians(45)):
    continue
```

By pre-filtering surface normals and enforcing near-vertical axis priors, the algorithm rejects 95% of branch and ground clutter before spending compute cycles on distance calculations.

The result? The solver converges in **under 12 milliseconds per stem** on edge hardware, letting us map forest stands with sub-centimeter accuracy while walking through the trees.
