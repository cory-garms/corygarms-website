Every few years, a committee of NCAA bureaucrats in Indianapolis convenes in an air-conditioned boardroom, reviews a decade-old paperwork infraction, and solemnly decrees that several games of football—witnessed live by 102,000 screaming people in Tiger Stadium and millions on television—*never actually happened*.

As an LSU alumnus who lived through the Les Miles era (grass-eating, fourth-down voodoo, and all), the concept of "vacating wins" has always struck me as an exercise in bureaucratic magical realism.

### The Database from Hell

If you look at college football through the eyes of a software engineer, the NCAA’s record-keeping is an absolute crime against database normalization:

* When Team A beats Team B 45–14, and Team A has a win vacated years later, Team A's win turns into a blank space.
* But Team B doesn't get a win. Nor is their loss erased.

In the global ledger of college athletics, the NCAA maintains a permanent set of historical games where **somebody lost, but nobody won**. Any database engineer who designed a production system with this kind of asymmetric referential integrity would be asked to clear out their desk by lunchtime.

### The $400 Plane Ticket vs. Modern NIL

The official justification was always "protecting competitive balance." The theory went that if a booster covered a $400 plane ticket for an athlete's family member, that player had an unfair athletic advantage when tackling an SEC running back.

Never mind that the player in question was a 6-foot-3, 240-pound future first-round NFL draft pick who ran a 4.4 forty. I can assure you it was not the delta airfare giving him an unfair advantage at the goal line.

Now we live in the era of Name, Image, and Likeness (NIL), where college sophomores drive sponsored trucks and sign six-figure brand deals before taking their first snap. Yet the NCAA still clings to historical asterisks over infractions that wouldn't cover the catering bill at a modern recruiting visit.

### You Can't Delete a Saturday Night

The beauty of sports is that they happen in physical reality. Leather crosses goal lines, clocks hit zero, and fireworks go off over Death Valley. 

You can edit a PDF on a server in Indiana. You can print asterisks in a media guide. But you can't vacate the sound of 100,000 fans singing "Callin' Baton Rouge" on a cool November night. That happened in real physics, and no committee memo has an API to roll that back.
